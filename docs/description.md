1. Executive Summary
Smartcare Africa Foundation is a Tanzanian non-governmental organization (NGO) formally registered under the Non-Governmental Organizations Act, No. 24 of 2002. The Foundation was established to confront a convergence of public health emergencies and environmental challenges concentrated in Biharamulo District, Kagera Region — a community grappling with the aftermath of the Marburg virus outbreak, endemic sanitation-related diseases, and entrenched cultural barriers to modern healthcare practices.
The organization operates with a clearly articulated constitutional structure encompassing 30 articles across seven parts, governing everything from its foundational identity and objectives, through membership rights, leadership roles, and financial stewardship, to conflict resolution and dissolution procedures. The Foundation's constitution has received formal approval from the Registrar of Non-Governmental Organizations, as evidenced by the official stamp appearing throughout the document.
This analysis provides a thorough examination of every constitutional provision of Smartcare Africa Foundation — assessing its governance architecture, evaluating the roles and responsibilities of its leadership, and identifying both strengths and areas for improvement. It is intended to serve as a reference document for members, stakeholders, prospective donors, and partner organizations seeking to understand the Foundation's structure and mandate.

2. Organizational Identity & Registration
2.1 Legal Name & Status
The organization is formally named Smartcare Africa Foundation. It is constituted as a Non-Governmental Organization under the laws of the United Republic of Tanzania, specifically under the Non-Governmental Organizations Act, No. 24 of 2002 and its subsequent amendments. This legal grounding gives the Foundation a recognized juridical personality, enabling it to enter contracts, open bank accounts, employ staff, and operate as an independent civic entity.

2.3 Area of Operation
Per Article 6, the Foundation is authorized to operate at the national level across Tanzania, not merely within Biharamulo District. This national mandate enables the Foundation to scale its programs, attract national-level funding, and build partnerships with organizations across all Tanzanian regions. While the immediate operational focus is on Biharamulo District — given the specific public health challenges articulated in the Preamble — the national scope provides significant long-term growth potential.
2.4 Founding Context & Preamble Analysis
The Preamble (Article 3) offers an unusually detailed justification for the Foundation's creation. It identifies four interlocking crises that made the formation of a dedicated NGO necessary:
Public Health Emergency: The Marburg virus outbreak in Biharamulo District represented a severe crisis. Marburg is a hemorrhagic fever with high fatality rates, and its occurrence in an area already burdened by endemic diseases (cholera, dysentery, typhoid) created a compounded public health emergency.
Environmental Degradation: Mismanaged livestock practices led to waste accumulation, surface and groundwater contamination, and soil degradation — directly threatening the health and livelihoods of community members.
Cultural & Behavioral Barriers: Cultural norms and practices in the community created resistance to modern hygiene standards and healthcare-seeking behavior, making public health interventions more complex than straightforward service delivery.
Infrastructure & Education Deficits: Limited access to information, inadequate sanitation infrastructure, and poor roads restricted both the availability of and access to health solutions.
This multi-causal analysis embedded in the Preamble is a mark of organizational sophistication. It signals that the Foundation's approach is not purely curative but addresses the structural determinants of poor health in the region.
3. Vision, Mission & Strategic Objectives
3.1 Vision Statement Analysis
The Vision Statement (Article 7) presents an environmentally integrative health philosophy. The Foundation envisions a world where health and environmental wellbeing are mutually reinforcing — explicitly connecting clean air, safe water, nutritious food, and quality healthcare as interlinked necessities rather than separate domains. This holistic framing aligns with global public health paradigms such as One Health, which recognizes the interdependence of human, animal, and environmental health.
Vision in Practice "We envision a world where health and the environment thrive together... promoting clean air, safe water, nutritious food and quality healthcare. By prioritizing conservation, innovation, and partnerships."
This vision is notably ambitious and global in aspiration, even as the Foundation's immediate mandate is community-specific in Biharamulo.
3.2 Mission Statement Analysis
The Mission Statement (Article 8) is action-oriented and clearly beneficiary-focused. It identifies the primary target group as disadvantaged populations and articulates five operational commitments:
Providing free or affordable healthcare to those who cannot access conventional health systems.
Promoting health education — targeted campaigns on hygiene, nutrition, maternal health, and disease prevention.
Supporting mental health initiatives and expanding access to mental health services, which are chronically under-resourced in rural Tanzania.
Reducing health disparities and actively working toward health equity between different population groups.
Advocating for policy reforms that improve healthcare access for vulnerable and marginalized communities.
The explicit inclusion of mental health is notable. In the Tanzanian context, mental health services are severely under-resourced, and the social stigma surrounding mental illness is significant. The Foundation's constitutional inclusion of mental health as a core mandate positions it to address a widely neglected dimension of community health.

4. Membership Structure & Governance Rights
4.1 Membership Eligibility (Article 10)
Membership is voluntary and open to any individual who has:
Attained the minimum age of 18 years;
Is of sound mind; and
Expresses willingness to support the Foundation's vision, mission, and objectives.
The constitution distinguishes between local and international NGO membership requirements — for a local NGO, all members must be Tanzanian nationals; for an international NGO, at least two of three founding members must be Tanzanian. This dual provision is forward-thinking: it accommodates potential future internationalisation of the Foundation while maintaining a strong Tanzanian foundation in the founding structure.
4.2 Three Categories of Members (Article 11)
The Foundation recognizes three distinct membership categories:
| Membership Category | Description |
| Founder Members | Those who took the initiative to establish the organization. They carry the foundational mandate and institutional memory of the Foundation. |
| Ordinary Members | Individuals who join after the Foundation's establishment. This is the primary growth pathway for expanding the membership base. |
| Honorary Members | Awarded at the discretion of the organization to individuals who have made exceptional contributions to the community and the Foundation's objectives. This category is non-voting but recognition-oriented. |
4.3 Rights and Obligations (Article 12)
The constitution balances member rights with clear obligations, reflecting a dual-accountability model. Members both have entitlements and bear responsibilities to the collective:
Member Rights
Electoral participation: The right to vote and stand for election in leadership positions (excluding honorary members).
Right to be heard: Formal recognition of the right to voice opinions within the organization.
Attendance at General Meetings: Full participation in the supreme decision-making body.
Access to information: Members have the right to receive updates about organizational progress through available communication channels.
Member Obligations
Active participation in all organizational activities as required.
Payment of fees as prescribed by the General Meeting — a financial commitment to organizational sustainability.
Attendance at all required meetings.
Maintaining confidentiality of organizational matters and decisions — a critical governance protection.
4.4 Cessation of Membership (Article 13)
Membership may cease under six defined circumstances:
Voluntary resignation from the organization.
Expulsion for misconduct or failure to comply with the constitution or organizational resolutions.
Absence from three consecutive General Meetings without providing reasonable notice or explanation.
Mental incapacity that prevents the member from fulfilling obligations.
Non-payment of subscription fees for one full year.
Death of the member.
These cessation provisions are comprehensive and fair — they provide clear grounds for exit (voluntary or involuntary) while protecting the organization from inactive or non-contributing members undermining its functioning.
5. Leadership: Office Bearers & Their Roles
The Foundation has three designated office bearers: the Chairman, Secretary, and Treasurer (Articles 14–16). These three individuals constitute the executive leadership of the organization and collectively serve as signatories to the organization's bank account. Together they form the operational backbone of day-to-day governance.
5.1 The Chairman (Article 14)
The Chairman is the highest-ranking office bearer and holds both ceremonial and executive responsibilities:
Presides over all General Meetings of the Organization, ensuring orderly deliberation and decision-making.
Serves as the official spokesman for the Foundation in all external communications, public forums, and stakeholder engagements.
Acts as a signatory to the bank account, providing financial oversight and ensuring that expenditures are properly authorized.
Oversees the daily activities of the Organization, functioning as the de facto Chief Executive in operational matters.
The Chairman's role combines strategic representation with operational oversight — a wide mandate that makes the selection of an appropriate person critical to organizational effectiveness.
5.2 The Secretary (Article 15)
The Secretary serves as the administrative and institutional memory of the Foundation. Specific responsibilities include:
Calling meetings in consultation with the Chairperson — ensuring proper scheduling and notice compliance.
Arranging the schedule of duties in the office — managing day-to-day administrative workflow.
Receiving and processing all incoming letters, including membership application letters, which are then submitted to the General Meeting for formal approval.
Keeping all organizational documents — making the Secretary the primary custodian of institutional records.
Serving as a signatory to the bank account — embedding the Secretary in financial authorization processes.
Maintaining records of all organizational assets.
Administrative Signatory Context Sabri Salumu Shabani is listed as Secretary of Smartcare Africa Foundation. As Secretary, Sabri holds both administrative custodianship and financial signatory authority — a dual responsibility that makes this a highly active and consequential office.
5.3 The Treasurer (Article 16)
The Treasurer is the financial steward of the organization with comprehensive accounting and reporting responsibilities:
Receives and safeguards all organizational funds.
Prepares the annual budget and financial estimates to be presented to the General Meeting for approval.
Maintains proper financial records including books of account and asset registers.
Prepares statements of income and expenditure, and submits these together with the audited financial report to the General Meeting.
Serves as one of three signatories to the bank account.
The Treasurer's role demands both technical financial competence and personal integrity. The provision requiring the submission of audited reports alongside financial statements reflects good governance practices — ensuring that financial accountability is externally verified, not purely self-reported.
5.4 Election & Term of Office (Article 17)
Office bearers are elected by the General Meeting from among the membership. The term of office is three (3) years, and office bearers may be re-elected for one additional consecutive term of three years. This six-year maximum term limit per consecutive cycle is an important governance feature that prevents indefinite concentration of power while still allowing experienced leaders to continue contributing.
6. Board of Directors: Composition & Duties
6.1 Composition (Article 18)
The Board of Directors is the primary governance body of the Foundation between General Meetings. It is composed of:
The Chairperson (ex officio)
The Executive Secretary (ex officio)
The Treasurer (ex officio)
Five additional members appointed by the General Meeting
This gives the Board a total of eight members. The inclusion of all three office bearers ensures executive-level accountability to the Board, while the five appointed members provide broader representation of the membership and different perspectives. Board members serve three-year terms and may be reappointed for one additional term.
Board Meeting Mandates The Board convenes at minimum twice per year. Emergency meetings can be called at any time when urgent matters arise. A quorum of 2/3 of Board members (i.e., at least 6 of 8) is required for any meeting to be valid.
6.2 Functions & Duties (Article 19)
The Board holds a broad mandate that encompasses governance, strategic planning, operational oversight, and institutional development:
Rule-making: The Board has authority to formulate rules and regulations governing organizational conduct and operations.
Brand & Reputation Management: Protecting and advancing the public image of the Foundation.
Recruitment of Office Bearers: The Board recruits individuals for executive leadership positions.
Strategic Planning: Active participation in developing the Foundation's strategic plans.
Audit Appointment: The Board appoints external auditors to ensure financial independence and accountability.
Project Development: Convening and overseeing projects, commissioning feasibility studies, and submitting approved projects to the General Meeting.
Committee Formation: Establishing specialized sub-committees as needed to handle specific organizational functions.
The Board's dual responsibility — governing the organization while also developing operational plans — is typical for small NGOs where the distinction between governance and management layers is necessarily blurred.
7. General Meeting: Supreme Decision-Making Body
7.1 Composition & Authority (Article 20)
The General Meeting is the highest organ of the Foundation. It is composed of all members and operates on a majority-vote basis. Its supreme authority means that no decision of the Board or office bearers can override a properly passed resolution of the General Meeting.
7.2 Powers and Functions (Article 21)
The General Meeting holds the following exclusive powers:
Membership governance: Discussing admissions of new members, disciplinary action, and expulsion of members.
Financial oversight: Discussing and approving annual budgets, physical and financial implementation reports.
Constitutional amendment: The sole body authorized to amend the Constitution.
Electoral authority: Electing all office bearers of the Foundation.
Board supervision: Approving all issues originating from the Board of Directors.
Board appointment: Appointing the members of the Board of Directors.
Auditor approval: Approving the appointment of external auditors.
The concentration of ultimate authority in the General Meeting is consistent with the democratic and participatory governance model appropriate for a membership-based NGO.
7.3 Annual General Meeting (Article 22)
The Annual General Meeting (AGM) is held once per year. Members must be given 30 days' advance notice, ensuring adequate preparation time. A quorum of two-thirds () of all members is required for the meeting to be valid. Decisions are by majority vote of members present.
7.4 Extra-Ordinary General Meeting (Article 23)
An Extra-Ordinary General Meeting (EOGM) may be convened at any time in cases of emergency or urgent organizational need. The notice period for an EOGM is significantly shorter — 7 days — reflecting the urgency that typically prompts such a meeting. The same  quorum requirement applies. This provision provides the Foundation with important agility in responding to unforeseen challenges or opportunities.

The Foundation's financial year runs from 1 January to 31 December of each calendar year. This standard financial year aligns with Tanzania's national government fiscal reporting cycle and makes audits and comparative reporting more straightforward.

8.3 Use of Funds (Article 26)
All funds, regardless of their source, must be applied exclusively toward the promotion of the Foundation's objectives. This restriction is a standard and important governance safeguard. It prevents mission drift and ensures that donor funds, member fees, and grants are not diverted to non-organizational purposes. Any expenditure that cannot be directly linked to organizational objectives would constitute a governance violation.
8.4 Bank Account & Transaction Authorization (Article 27)
A bank account is to be opened in the name of the Foundation. All three office bearers — the Chairman, Secretary, and Treasurer — are designated as signatories. Critically, any financial transaction requires the signatures of at least two of the three signatories. This dual-signature requirement is an essential financial control measure that prevents unilateral financial decisions and protects against fraud or misappropriation.
Financial Control Highlight The requirement for two signatories per transaction is a best-practice governance measure. It ensures checks and balances in financial operations and is commonly required by major institutional donors (UN agencies, international foundations) as a prerequisite for grant eligibility.
9. Constitutional Provisions & Dissolution
9.1 Constitutional Amendments (Article 28)
The Constitution may be amended through a resolution passed at the General Meeting by a majority vote of eligible members. Any article, clause, or part of the Constitution may be removed, added to, or amended through this process. The requirement for General Meeting approval — rather than Board approval — ensures that fundamental changes to the organization's governing document reflect the broader membership's will, not merely that of the leadership.
Notably, the Constitution does not specify a supermajority (e.g., two-thirds) for constitutional amendments — a simple majority suffices. This lowers the bar for constitutional change, which can be both flexible and potentially destabilizing. Future revisions might consider requiring a higher threshold for fundamental constitutional changes.
9.2 Dissolution (Article 29)
The Foundation may be dissolved through one of two mechanisms:
By a resolution passed at the General Meeting with a minimum two-thirds () majority of eligible voters.
By operation of law — e.g., through regulatory action, court order, or statutory deregistration.
Upon dissolution, after all debts have been settled, remaining assets must be transferred to another NGO registered under the same Act and with similar objectives. This asset transfer provision is both a legal requirement under Tanzanian NGO law and an ethical commitment to ensuring that community-focused assets remain within the social sector. It prevents the private appropriation of organizational assets upon closure.
9.3 Conflict Resolution (Article 30)
The Constitution establishes a two-stage conflict resolution process:
Primary Resolution: The Board of Directors is the first point of escalation for any internal conflict. This positions the Board as an internal tribunal with authority to mediate and resolve disputes.
Escalated Resolution: If the Board fails to resolve the dispute, the matter is referred to the General Meeting — the supreme organ — for final determination.
This tiered approach is sensible and aligns with good governance practices. However, the Constitution does not address conflicts involving the Board itself (i.e., where Board members are parties to the dispute), nor does it provide for external mediation or arbitration as a fallback.

11. Strengths, Gaps & Recommendations
11.1 Constitutional Strengths
The Foundation's constitution demonstrates several notable governance strengths:
Dual-Signature Financial Control: The requirement for two of three signatories on any bank transaction is an excellent financial safeguard. It reduces risk of misappropriation and meets the requirements of most institutional donors.
Comprehensive Preamble: The detailed Preamble (Article 3) goes beyond a simple statement of intent — it provides epidemiological and social context justifying the organization's existence. This strengthens grant applications and donor communications.
Tiered Membership: Three membership categories (Founder, Ordinary, Honorary) provide flexible pathways for community engagement, from formal voting members to recognized supporters.
Asset Protection on Dissolution: The constitutional requirement to transfer remaining assets to another NGO with similar objectives ensures organizational assets remain in the social sector even in the event of closure.
National Scope with Local Roots: Operating nationally while being rooted in Biharamulo gives the Foundation flexibility to scale while maintaining community authenticity.
11.2 Identified Gaps
Several areas present opportunities for constitutional strengthening:
Gap 1: Spelling Inconsistencies The word "Treasurer" is consistently spelled "Tresurer" throughout the Constitution (Articles 2, 16, and the Members table). While this does not affect legal validity, it is a credibility concern when submitting the document to international donors, government agencies, or audit firms.
Gap 2: No Supermajority for Constitutional Amendments Article 28 allows constitutional changes by a simple majority vote. International best practice for NGO constitutions typically requires a supermajority (e.g.,  or ) for constitutional amendments, to prevent instability or short-term majorities from making fundamental changes.
Gap 3: No External Mediation Provision for Conflict Resolution Article 30 provides for Board-level then General Meeting-level dispute resolution, but does not address: (a) conflicts in which the Board itself is the subject of the dispute, or (b) any mechanism for external arbitration or mediation.
Gap 4: Incomplete Vision Statement (Article 7) The Vision Statement in Article 7 appears incomplete in the raw layout. It begins with "To provide free or affordable healthcare to disadvantaged populations, promote health education, support mental" — and then is cut off. The complete vision statement should be formally completed in the next constitutional review.
Gap 5: Quorum Risk for Small Membership With only four founding members currently listed, the  quorum requirement for both AGMs and EOGMs means at least 3 members must be present. As the organization grows, this is manageable. However, before significant membership expansion, any absence could risk inquorate meetings.
Gap 6: No Remuneration Policy The Constitution does not address whether office bearers and Board members may receive remuneration, allowances, or expense reimbursements for their service. Absence of this provision creates ambiguity and potential future conflict.

12. Conclusion
Smartcare Africa Foundation stands as a well-intentioned, constitutionally grounded non-governmental organization with a meaningful mandate in one of Tanzania's most underserved health landscapes. Established in response to genuine public health crises — including the Marburg virus outbreak and chronic environmental health challenges in Biharamulo District — the Foundation is built on a foundation of genuine community need.
Its 30-article constitution covers the full spectrum of organizational governance: from legal identity and membership structure, through clearly defined leadership roles and a functioning Board of Directors, to comprehensive financial management provisions and a sound conflict resolution framework. The dual-signature bank account requirement, the tiered membership system, the asset protection clause in the dissolution provisions, and the separation of AGM and EOGM processes all reflect solid governance thinking.
The Foundation's vision — a world where health and environment thrive together — is both ambitious and timely. Its six strategic objectives balance direct service delivery with systemic advocacy, positioning it as an organization with both immediate community impact and long-term transformative aspirations. The explicit commitment to mental health services, so often overlooked in low-resource health programming, is particularly commendable.
The identified gaps — typographic inconsistencies, an incomplete Vision Statement article, absence of a remuneration policy, and limitations in the conflict resolution framework — are all addressable through deliberate constitutional review and Board resolutions. They do not undermine the Foundation's legitimacy but represent natural areas of growth for a newly established organization.
With four committed founding members, a registered legal status, national operational scope, and a clearly articulated theory of change, Smartcare Africa Foundation is positioned to grow into a significant health and wellness institution within Tanzania. The journey from founding document to field impact requires continued organizational development, membership recruitment, resource mobilization, and programmatic planning — but the constitutional groundwork is solid.
Final Assessment
Smartcare Africa Foundation has a sound governance constitution befitting its mission. With targeted refinements at the next General Meeting and a strategic programmatic plan, the Foundation is well-positioned to attract donor support, build community trust, and deliver meaningful health impact in Biharamulo District and beyond.
